\section{Representation variety}
For this chapter we mostly follow \cite{maret_note_nodate, casimiroCharacterVarietiesTheir2026}. Throughout, $\Gamma$ denotes a finitely generated group and $G$ a (real or complex) Lie group.


\begin{definition}
    The \emph{representation variety} of $\Gamma$ in $G$ is the set
    \[
       \RepVar{\Gamma}{G}:= \Hom(\Gamma, G) = \{ \rho : \Gamma \to G \mid \rho \text{ is a group homomorphism} \}.
    \]
    Its elements are called \emph{representations}.
\end{definition}

We endow $\RepVar{\Gamma}{G}$ with the subspace topology induced from the compact-open topology on the space of continuous maps $\Gamma \to G$, where $\Gamma$ carries the discrete topology. Since the compact subsets of a discrete space are the finite ones, a subbasis for this topology is given by the sets
\[
    V(K, U) = \{ \rho \in \RepVar{\Gamma}{G} : \rho(K) \subseteq U \},
\]
where $K \subseteq \Gamma$ is finite and $U \subseteq G$ is open.

Fix a set of generators $\{\gamma_1, \dots, \gamma_n\}$ of $\Gamma$. It is customary to identify the representation variety with the space of tuples of images of the generators,
\[
    X(\Gamma, G) = \{ (\rho(\gamma_1), \dots, \rho(\gamma_n)) : \rho \in \RepVar{\Gamma}{G} \} \subseteq G^n.
\]
The following proposition justifies both this identification and the name ``variety''.


\begin{proposition} \label{prop:analytic-structure}
 If $G$ is a Lie group endowed with an analityc atlas then $X(\Gamma,G)$ is an analytic subvariety of $G^n$, homeomorphic to $\Hom(\Gamma,G)$. In particular $\Hom(\Gamma,G)$ inherits a natural structure of analityc variety wich is independent of the choice of generators of $\Gamma$.  	
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
	Let $\{\gamma_{1}, \dots, \gamma_n\}$ be a set of generators of $\Gamma$, and let $r_i$ denote the set of relations for these generators. Recall that every group homomorphis is defined by the image of its generators, and those images have to preserve the relations between the generators. Each relation can be thought as a map $r_i:G^n \to G$, since this map is just a composition of inversions and multiplications, then each $r_i$ is analytic. Then $X(\Gamma,G)$ is precisely the subset of $(g_{1},\dots,g_n) \in G^n$ defined by the conditions $r_i(g_{1},\dots,g_n)=1$. It is clear that we have a bijection.
	\[
		\Psi: \Hom(\Gamma,G) \to X(\Gamma,G), \quad \rho \to (\rho(\gamma_{1}),\dots,\rho(\gamma_n))
	\]
To see that $\Psi$ is a homeomorphism, consider $U_{1},\dots,U_n$ open sets in $G$. Then 
\[
	\Psi^{-1}(X(\Gamma,G)\cap \left(U_1 \times \dots \times U_n\right)) = \Hom(\Gamma,G) \cap \bigcap_{i=1}^n V \left(\{\gamma_i)\}, U_i \right)
\]
This proves $\Psi$ is continuous. Now for its inverse, take $K \subset \Gamma$ finite and $U \subset G$ open. Any $\gamma \in K$ can be expressed as a word in the generators, and hence it determines an analytic map $\gamma:G^n \to G $, hence
\[
	\Psi(\Hom(\Gamma,G)\cap V(K,U)) = X(\Gamma,G) \cap \bigcap_{\gamma \in K} \gamma ^{-1}(U)
\]
Therefore, $\Psi$ is a homeomorphism.

If $(\gamma'_{1},\dots,\gamma'_n)$ is another set of generators of $\Gamma$ and $X'(\Gamma,G)$ is the associated space, then the map from $X(\Gamma,G)$ to $X'(\Gamma,G)$ defined as the composition 
\[
	X(\Gamma,G) \to \Hom(\Gamma,G) \to X'(\Gamma,G)
\]is an isomorphism of analytic varieties. Indeed, the map sends $(\rho(\gamma_{1}),\dots,\rho(\gamma_n))$ to $(\rho(\gamma_{1}'),\dots,\rho(\gamma_n))$ . Now, since $\gamma_i'$ is a word in the generators $\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_n$, it follows that $\rho(\gamma_i')$ is a word in $\rho(\gamma_{1}),\dots,\rho(\gamma_n)$. This shows that the map is analytic because word maps are analytic by assumption on $G$.

\end{proof}
\begin{proposition}
	Assume that $G$ has the structure of a real or complex algebraic group, then $X(\Gamma,G)$ is an algebraic subset of $G^n$. In particular,$\Hom(\Gamma,G) $has a natural structure of real or complex algebraic variety and the structure does not depend on the choice of generators of $\Gamma$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
    The proof is analogous to that of Proposition~\ref{prop:analytic-structure}, replacing ``analytic'' by ``regular'': word maps are regular because multiplication and inversion in $G$ are.
\end{proof}




The representation variety $\Hom(\Gamma,G)$, carries two natural symmetries given by the right action of the group $\operatorname{Aut}(\Gamma)$ of automorphisms of $\Gamma$ by pre-composition and the left action of $\operatorname{Aut}(G)$ by post-composition. Both actions preserve the analytic or algebraic structure of $\Hom(\Gamma,G)$ as a consequence of the next proposition
\begin{proposition}
	Let $\Gamma, \Gamma_{1},\Gamma_{2}$ be finitely generated groups, and let $G,G_{1},G_{2}$ be Lie or algebraic groups.
	\begin{enumerate}
	   \item If $f:\Gamma_{1} \to \Gamma_{2}$ is a homomorphism, then the induced map $ f^* : \Hom(\Gamma_{2}, G) \to \Hom(\Gamma_{1}, G),\quad \rho \mapsto \rho \circ f$  is analytic (or regular). 
	   \item If $\phi:G_{1}\to G_{2}$ is a homomorphism, then the induced map $ \iphi_* : \Hom(\Gamma, G_{1}) \to \Hom(\Gamma, G_{2}), \rho \mapsto \phi \circ \rho$ is analytic (or regular).		   
	\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
	See \cite[Lemma 1.2.7]{maret_note_nodate}
\end{proof} 

Among $\operatorname{Aut(G)}$ normal subgroups, one is particularly important, the subgroup of inner automorphisms $\operatorname{Inn}(G)$ defined by conjugation with elements of $G$. In  particular, $\Inn(G)=G/Z(G)$. The action of $\operatorname{Inn}(G)$ on $\Hom(\Gamma, G)$ is fundamental in many geometric situations: for example, holonomy representations of surface structures are only well-defined up to conjugation by G. This motivates the study of the quotient $ \operatorname{Hom}(\Gamma, G)/\operatorname{Inn}(G)$,


\subsection{Characterizations of the orbits of the $\operatorname{Inn}(G)$-action }
In this subsection we look for subsets of the representation variety on which the conjugation action behaves well enough to yield a well-behaved quotient.

For a representation $\rho \in \RepVar{\Gamma}{G}$ we write $Z(\rho) := Z(\rho(\Gamma))$ for the centralizer of its image. Note that $Z(G) \subseteq Z(\rho)$ always, and that $Z(c_g \cdot \rho) = g Z(\rho) g^{-1}$.

\begin{definition}
    A representation $\rho \in \RepVar{\Gamma}{G}$ is called \emph{regular} if $\dim Z(\rho) = \dim Z(G)$, and \emph{very regular} if $Z(\rho) = Z(G)$. We denote by $\Homreg(\Gamma, G)$ and $\Homvreg(\Gamma, G)$ the subsets of regular and very regular representations, respectively.
\end{definition}

Both subsets are $\Inn(G)$-invariant: conjugation preserves dimensions, and $g Z(G) g^{-1} = Z(G)$ for every $g \in G$.
 
\begin{lemma}\label{lem:free-action}
    The action of $\Inn(G)$ on $\Homvreg(\Gamma, G)$ is free.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
    Let $\rho \in \Homvreg(\Gamma, G)$ and suppose that $c_g \cdot \rho = \rho$, that is, $g \rho(\gamma) g^{-1} = \rho(\gamma)$ for all $\gamma \in \Gamma$. Then $g \in Z(\rho) = Z(G)$, so $c_g(h) = ghg^{-1} = h$ for every $h \in G$. Hence $c_g$ is the identity of $\Inn(G)$.
\end{proof}

 
\begin{proposition}[\cite{goldmanSymplecticNatureFundamental1984}]\label{prop:locally-free}
    The action of $\Inn(G)$ on $\Hom(\Gamma, G)$ is locally free at $\rho$ if and only if $\dim Z(\rho) = \dim Z(G)$, i.e.\ if and only if $\rho$ is regular.
\end{proposition}


\begin{proof}
  See	\cite[Proposition 8]{casimiroCharacterVarietiesTheir2026}.
\end{proof}

Our next desired property is properness.

\begin{definition}
	A proper group action is a continuous group action of a topological group $G$ on topological space $X$ for wich the map \[
		G \times X \to X\times X, (g,x) \to (gx,x)
	\]
	is a proper map, i.e., inverse images of compact sets are compact.
\end{definition}

Properness guarantees that the quotient behaves well topologically, as the next proposition shows.

\begin{proposition}
	If a topological group $G$ operates properly on a topological space $X$, then the orbit space $X/G$ is Hausdorff. If also $G$ is Hausdorff, then $X$ is Hausdorff.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
	See \cite[Prop. 3]{Bourbaki1967-qa}
\end{proof}


\begin{definition}
	Let $G$ be an algebraic group. A Borel subgroup of $G$ is a maximal, Zariski closed, solvable connected subgroup of $G$. A Zariski closed subgroup that contains a Borel subgroup is called a parabolic subgroup.

\end{definition}




\begin{definition}
	Let $G$ be an algebraic group. A representation $\phi: \Gamma \to G$ is called irreducible if the image of $\phi$ does not lie in a proper parabolic subgroup of $G$. We denote by $\Hom ^{\text{irr}}(\Gamma,G)$ the $\Inn(G)-$invariant subspace of irreducible representations.
\end{definition}


\begin{theorem}\label{thm:proper-action}
	Let $G$ be a reductive algebraic group. The $\Inn(G)$-action on $\Hom ^{\text{irr}}(\Gamma,G)$  is proper.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
    See \cite[Prop.~1.1]{JohnsonDennis1987DSAt}.
\end{proof}

If we add freeness to properness, we obtain an analytic structure on the quotient.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:analytic-quotient}
    Let $G$ be an analytic Lie group acting analytically, properly, and freely on an analytic manifold $X$. Then the orbit space $X/G$, endowed with the quotient topology, is a Hausdorff space. Furthermore, there exists a unique analytic manifold structure on $X/G$ such that the canonical projection $\pi: X \to X/G$ is an analytic submersion.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
	See \cite[Chap.~III, \S 1.5, Prop.~10]{Bourbaki1975-gf}
\end{proof}

To obtain an analytic quotient we may therefore use the representations that are both irreducible and very regular. This motivates the following definition.

\begin{definition}
    Let $G$ be an algebraic group. A representation $\rho : \Gamma \to G$ is called \emph{good} if it is irreducible and very regular. We denote by $\Homgd(\Gamma, G)$ the $\Inn(G)$-invariant subset of good representations.
\end{definition}


By Lemma~\ref{lem:free-action} and Theorem~\ref{thm:proper-action}, the conjugation action on $\Homgd(\Gamma, G)$ is both free and proper. Hence, whenever $\Homgd(\Gamma, G)$ is a manifold, the quotient by conjugation inherits the structure of an analytic manifold. This happens, for instance, for closed surface groups.

\begin{proposition}[\cite{goldmanSymplecticNatureFundamental1984, sikoraCharacterVarieties2012}]\label{prop:good-surface}
    Let $G$ be a reductive algebraic group.
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item $\Homgd(\Gamma, G)$ is Zariski open in $\Hom(\Gamma, G)$.
        \item If $\Gamma = \pi_{g,0}$, then:
        \begin{enumerate}
            \item $\Homgd(\pi_{g,0}, G)$ is an analytic manifold of dimension $(2g-1)\dim G + \dim Z(G)$;
            \item the conjugation action on $\Homgd(\pi_{g,0}, G)$ is free and proper;
            \item the quotient $\Homgd(\pi_{g,0}, G) / \Inn(G)$ is an analytic manifold of dimension $(2g-2)\dim G + 2\dim Z(G)$, which is always even.
        \end{enumerate}
    \end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

If we only want a Hausdorff quotient, we can relax the irreducibility condition, as we shall see.

\begin{definition}
    An algebraic group is called \emph{linearly reductive} if all its finite-dimensional representations are completely reducible, i.e. decompose as direct sums of simple subrepresentations. A subgroup of an algebraic group is called \emph{completely reducible} if its Zariski closure is linearly reductive.
\end{definition}

Over $\RR$ or $\CC$, an algebraic group is linearly reductive if and only if its Zariski identity component is reductive (see \cite[Corollary 22.43]{milneAlgebraicGroupsTheory2017}). This equivalence allows us to use both notions interchangeably in most geometric contexts.

\begin{definition}
    Let $G$ be an algebraic group. A representation $\rho : \Gamma \to G$ is called \emph{reductive} (or \emph{completely reducible}) if $\rho(\Gamma) \subseteq G$ is completely reducible. We denote by $\Homred(\Gamma, G)$ the $\Inn(G)$-invariant subset of reductive representations.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[\cite{florentinoTopologyModuliSpaces2009, sikoraCharacterVarieties2012}]\label{prop:closed-orbits}
    Let $G$ be a reductive algebraic group. A representation $\rho : \Gamma \to G$ is reductive if and only if its conjugation orbit $\mathcal{O}_\rho$ is closed in $\Hom(\Gamma, G)$.
\end{proposition}
 
As a consequence of Proposition~\ref{prop:closed-orbits}, the topological quotient $\Homred(\Gamma, G) / \Inn(G)$ is a $T_1$-space. In fact, it is Hausdorff.

\begin{proposition}
	Let $G$ be a reductive algebraic group. The topological quotient \[
		\Homred(\Gamma,G)/\Inn(G)
	\]
	is Haussdorf.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
    See \cite[\S 7.3]{richardsonMinimumVectorsReal1990}.
\end{proof}

\section{GIT Quotient}

As we saw in the previous section, the conjugation action of $\Inn(G)$ on $\Hom(\Gamma, G)$ yields an analytic, or at least Hausdorff, quotient only under certain conditions, and these conditions rarely hold on the whole representation variety. A first remedy is to restrict to reductive representations: the quotient $\Homred(\Gamma, G)/\Inn(G)$ is Hausdorff, although it is merely a topological space. On the other hand, $\Hom(\Gamma, G)$ is also an algebraic variety, and this additional structure is lost when passing to the topological quotient. In this section we construct a quotient that retains the algebraic structure, using the tools of geometric invariant theory (GIT).


\nocite{zamorasaizGeometricInvariantTheory2021}

We start by noticing that the $G$-action on $X$ induces an action on the ring of regular functions $k[X]$: for $g \in G$ and $f \in k[X]$, let $f^g \in k[X]$ be the function
\[
    f^g(x) := f(g \cdot x).
\]
Since $(f^g)^h = f^{gh}$, this is a right action of $G$ on $k[X]$.
 
For our purpose of constructing an algebraic quotient, we focus on the functions that are constant on the orbits.
 
\begin{definition}[Invariant functions]
    The \emph{ring of invariant functions} of the action is
    \[
        k[X]^G := \{ f \in k[X] : f^g = f \text{ for all } g \in G \}.
    \]
\end{definition}


Equivalently, $k[X]^G$ consists of the regular functions $f$ satisfying $f(g \cdot x) = f(x)$ for all $g \in G$ and $x \in X$. A key result in this subject is the following theorem.
 
\begin{theorem}[Nagata]\label{thm:nagata}
    Let $G$ be a reductive algebraic group acting regularly on an affine variety $X$. Then the ring of invariants $k[X]^G$ is a finitely generated $k$-algebra.
\end{theorem}
Nagata's original result is somewhat more general and can be found in \cite{nagataInvariantsGroupAffine1963}.

Since $X$ is irreducible, $k[X]$ is an integral domain, and so is its subring $k[X]^G$. Hence, by Theorem~\ref{thm:nagata} and Proposition~\ref{prop:kalgebras-affinevar}, $k[X]^G$ is the algebra of regular functions of some affine variety.

\begin{definition}[GIT quotient]\label{def:git-quotient}
	Using the maximal spectrum introduced in Subsection~\ref{subsec:Specmax}, the \emph{GIT\footnote{GIT stands for Geometric Invariant Theory} quotient} of $X$ by $G$ is
    \[
        X \git G := \Specmax\big(k[X]^G\big).
    \]
\end{definition}

By Proposition~\ref{prop:kalgebras-affinevar} and Proposition~\ref{prop:specmax-homeo}, $X \git G$ is (homeomorphic to) an affine variety whose ring of regular functions is $k[X]^G$.

    Some texts define the GIT quotient as $\operatorname{Spec}\big(k[X]^G\big)$ (cf.\ \cite{zamorasaizGeometricInvariantTheory2021, maret_note_nodate, casimiroCharacterVarietiesTheir2026}). In our setting, where $k[X]^G$ is a finitely generated $k$-algebra that is an integral domain, both constructions carry the same information: $\operatorname{Spec}(k[X]^G)$ is an affine scheme whose closed points form $\Specmax(k[X]^G)$, so the former is viewed as an affine scheme and the latter as an affine variety.

\section{Character variety}

By Nagata's theorem, when $G$ is reductive, the ring of invariant functions is a finitely generated algebra, then we can make the following definition

\begin{definition}
	The $G-$character variety is defined as $$
		\mathcal{X}_\Gamma(G) := \Hom(\Gamma,G) \git G = \operatorname{Specmax}\bigl(\CC[\Hom(\Gamma,G)]^G\bigr)
	$$
\end{definition}


The GIT character variety has by definition the structure of an algebraic variety and is, in particular, a Hausdorff topological space with the standard topology (see \cite[Lemma~3.3.3]{maret_note_nodate}. 




The name character varieties comes from the fact that these varieties are generated by characters. Given a representation $\rho \in \RepVar{\Gamma}{G}$, the character of the representation is \[
	\chi_ \rho: \Gamma \to \CC, \quad \gamma \mapsto \operatorname{tr}(\rho(\gamma)) 
\]

Indeed, in \cite{procesiInvariantTheoryMatrices1976}, Procesi proved the following result:

\begin{theorem}
	Let $M_m(K)$ the algebra of $m \times m$ matrices with coefficients in K, let $M_m(K)^n= M_m(K)\times \dots \times M_m(K)$ and $K[M_m(K)^n]$ be the algebra of regular functions in $\xi_k = (x^k_{i,j})_{i,j=1,\dots,m}$. The following hold:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item If $G \in \{GL(m, K), SL(m, K)\}$, then $K[M_m(K)^n]^G$ is finitely generated by trace polynomials $\operatorname{Tr}(W)$, where $W$ is a reduced word in $\xi_1, \dots, \xi_n$ of length at most $2^m - 1$.
    
    \item If $G \in \{O(m, K), SO(m, K)\}$, then $K[M_m(K)^n]^G$ is finitely generated by trace polynomials $\operatorname{Tr}(W)$, where $W$ is a reduced word of length at most $2^m - 1$ in $\xi_1, \dots, \xi_n$ and their orthogonal transposes.
    
    \item If $G = Sp(2m, K)$, then $K[M_{2m}(K)^n]^G$ is finitely generated by trace polynomials $\operatorname{Tr}(W)$, where $W$ is a reduced word of length at most $2^{2m} - 1$ in $\xi_1, \dots, \xi_n$ and their symplectic transposes.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

There is an alternative description of the GIT character variety that involves the notion of stability for representations.

\begin{definition}
	Let $G$ be an algebraic group and $\Gamma$ and a finitely generated group. A representation $\rho: \Gamma \to G$ is called
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item polystable if its orbit $\mathcal{O}_ \rho$ is closed.
		\item stable if its polystable and regular. 
	\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

We denote by $\RepVar{\Gamma}{G}{ps}$ (resp. $\RepVar{\Gamma}{G}{s}$) the subsets of polystable (resp. stable) representations wich are invariant under conjugation. When $G$ is a reductive algebraic group, a representation $\rho \in \RepVar{\Gamma}{G}{}$ is polystable if its reductive and it is stable if and only if it is irreducible. The following result then holds:

\begin{theorem}
Let G be a reductive complex algebraic group. Then there is a homeomorphism  Homps(Γ, G)/Inn(G) = Homred(Γ, G)/Inn(G) Hom(Γ, G)//G,  and Hom(Γ, G)//G contains the Zariski open subset Homs(Γ, G)/Inn(G) = Homirr(Γ, G)/Inn(G)
\end{theorem}

\section{Non Abelian Hodge Correspondence}

There is a particular family of character varieties, that is the Betti character or also known as Betti moduli space. Moduli spaces are structures that classify objects under some equivalence relation. The Betti moduli space is of special relevance for its rrelation with the Dolbeault moduli space: an space that paramaetrizes G-Higgs bundles and the De Rham space, a space that paramaetrizes flat bundles. 


For this section, let $X$ be a compact Kahler manifold. If we take $\Gamma= \pi(X)$ as the fundamental group of $X$ and $G$ a reductive Lie group 
\begin{definition}
	we define its Betti $G-$character variety or Betti moduli space as:
\[
	\mathcal{M}_B(X,G) = \mathcal{X}_{\pi(X)}(G)  
\]
\end{definition}

This space classifies the representations of the fundamental group up to conjugation

The Riemann-Hilbert correspondence [5,26] gives an analytic isomorphism (but not an algebraic isomorphism) Dd (G) ∼= Md (G).
\end{definition}
