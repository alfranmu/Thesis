\section{Conventions and Notation}

Let $k$ denote an algebraically closed field of characteristic zero. We will use the letter $e$ to denote the identity element of a group, and $T_pM$ for the tangent space of a manifold $M$ at a point $p$.

\section{Lie Groups and Their Lie Algebras}

A \emph{Lie group} $G$ is a group endowed with the additional structure of a smooth manifold, such that the group operations of multiplication and inversion are smooth maps. A \emph{homomorphism} $\varphi : G_1 \to G_2$ of Lie groups is a map that is both a group homomorphism and smooth as a map of manifolds.

\begin{definition}
    A \emph{Lie subgroup} of a Lie group $G$ is an injective homomorphism of Lie groups $i : H \to G$.
\end{definition}

Every Lie group admits an analytic atlas, unique up to analytic diffeomorphism, with respect to which multiplication and inversion are analytic maps (cf.\ \cite[Prop.\ 1.117]{KnappAnthonyW.1996LGBa}). The standard examples of Lie groups are $\GL_n(\RR)$, $\GL_n(\bC)$, and their closed subgroups, which are called \emph{linear Lie groups}.

Let $g$ be an arbitrary element of the Lie group $G$. We write $L_g$ for left translation by $g$, that is, $L_g(h) = gh$ for all $h \in G$. The group axioms imply that $L_g$ is a diffeomorphism with inverse $(L_g)^{-1} = L_{g^{-1}}$, so its differential $dL_g : TG \to TG$ is likewise a diffeomorphism; in particular, for each $h \in G$, the restriction $dL_g : T_hG \to T_{gh}G$ is a linear isomorphism.

\begin{definition}
    A vector field $X$ on $G$ is \emph{left-invariant} if
    \[
        dL_g(X_h) = X_{gh} \qquad \text{for all } g, h \in G.
    \]
\end{definition}

A left-invariant vector field is completely determined by its value at the identity: given $v \in T_eG$, the assignment $X_g := dL_g(v)$ defines a smooth left-invariant vector field on $G$.

\begin{proposition}
    The map $X \mapsto X_e$ is a linear isomorphism between the space of left-invariant vector fields on $G$ and the tangent space $T_eG$ at the identity.
\end{proposition}

The \emph{Lie algebra} of $G$, denoted $\mathfrak{g}$, is the space of left-invariant vector fields on $G$ endowed with the Lie bracket induced by the commutator of vector fields (see \cite[Ch.\ 2, \S 3]{milneLAG}). By the proposition above, $\mathfrak{g}$ may be identified with $T_eG$.

We now recall the notion of a one-parameter group of diffeomorphisms.

\begin{definition}
    A \emph{one-parameter group of diffeomorphisms} of a manifold $M$ is a smooth map $\rho : M \times \RR \to M$ such that, for all $x \in M$ and $s, t \in \RR$,
    \[
        \rho(x, s+t) = \rho(\rho(x,t), s).
    \]
\end{definition}

A one-parameter group can be viewed as a family of curves through each point $x \in M$; differentiating these curves produces a global vector field on $M$. Conversely, by the fundamental theorem of ordinary differential equations, every smooth vector field on $M$ generates a \emph{local} one-parameter group of diffeomorphisms, namely its flow. When the vector field is complete, this flow is defined for all time.

\begin{lemma}
    Every left-invariant vector field on a Lie group $G$ is complete (see \cite[Thm.\ 8.3.1]{barden2003introduction}). Consequently, its flow generates a one-parameter group of diffeomorphisms of $G$ defined for all $t \in \RR$.
\end{lemma}

For any left-invariant vector field $X \in \mathfrak{g}$, the integral curve of its flow through the identity is itself a one-parameter subgroup---that is, a smooth homomorphism $\gamma_X : \RR \to G$ with $\gamma_X'(0) = X_e$. This correspondence naturally motivates the definition of the exponential map.

\begin{definition}
    Let $X \in \mathfrak{g}$ and let $\gamma_X : \RR \to G$ be the one-parameter subgroup generated by $X$. The \emph{Lie exponential map} $\exp : \mathfrak{g} \to G$ is defined by
    \[
        \exp(X) = \gamma_X(1).
    \]
\end{definition}

The exponential map restricts to a diffeomorphism between a neighborhood of $0$ in $\mathfrak{g}$ and a neighborhood of $e$ in $G$.

For a linear Lie group, the Lie exponential map coincides with the ordinary matrix exponential: for $X$ in the corresponding Lie algebra,
\[
    \exp(X) = e^X = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \frac{X^k}{k!},
\]
with the series converging absolutely for every matrix $X$.

The group $G$ acts on itself by conjugation, yielding for each $g \in G$ an inner automorphism $C_g : G \to G$, $C_g(h) = ghg^{-1}$. Taking the differential of this action at the identity yields a linear map $\mathrm{Ad}_g := (dC_g)_e : \mathfrak{g} \to \mathfrak{g}$, and the assignment $g \mapsto \mathrm{Ad}_g$ defines a homomorphism $\mathrm{Ad} : G \to \mathrm{Aut}(\mathfrak{g})$, known as the \emph{adjoint representation} of $G$. Explicitly, for $g \in G$ and $\xi \in \mathfrak{g}$,
\[
    \mathrm{Ad}(g)(\xi) = \left.\frac{d}{dt}\right|_{t=0} g \exp(t\xi) g^{-1}.
\]

Following \cite{maret_note_nodate}, we classify Lie algebras as follows.

\begin{definition}
    Let $\mathfrak{g}$ be a Lie algebra.
    \begin{itemize}
        \item $\mathfrak{g}$ is \textbf{simple} if it is non-abelian and its only ideals are $0$ and $\mathfrak{g}$ itself. Equivalently, $\mathfrak{g}$ is simple if and only if its adjoint representation is irreducible and $\mathfrak{g}$ is not one-dimensional abelian.
        \item $\mathfrak{g}$ is \textbf{semisimple} if it has no nonzero abelian ideals; equivalently, if it decomposes as a direct sum of simple Lie algebras. The \emph{Killing form}
        \[
            K(v_1, v_2) := \operatorname{Tr}\big(\operatorname{ad}(v_1)\operatorname{ad}(v_2)\big), \qquad v_1, v_2 \in \mathfrak{g},
        \]
        is a symmetric bilinear form on $\mathfrak{g}$ that is non-degenerate precisely when $\mathfrak{g}$ is semisimple.
        \item $\mathfrak{g}$ is \textbf{reductive} if it is a direct sum of a semisimple Lie algebra and an abelian Lie algebra; equivalently, if its adjoint representation is completely reducible.
    \end{itemize}
\end{definition}

A connected Lie group is called simple, semisimple, or reductive according to whether its Lie algebra possesses the corresponding property. Every simple Lie group is semisimple, and every semisimple Lie group is reductive.

\section{Algebraic Varieties and Algebraic Groups}

The \emph{affine $n$-space} over $k$, denoted $\mathbb{A}_k^n$ or simply $\mathbb{A}^n$, is the set of all $n$-tuples of elements of $k$.

\begin{definition}
    Let $T \subseteq k[x_1, \dots, x_n]$ be a set of polynomials. The \emph{zero locus} of $T$ is
    \[
        Z(T) = \{ x \in \mathbb{A}^n : f(x) = 0 \text{ for all } f \in T \}.
    \]
\end{definition}

Sets of this form are called \emph{algebraic varieties}\footnote{Most of the literature refers to these as \emph{algebraic sets}; here we follow the convention of \cite{maret_note_nodate, casimiroCharacterVarietiesTheir2026}.}. It is straightforward to verify that algebraic varieties form the closed sets of a topology on $\mathbb{A}^n$, called the \emph{Zariski topology}.

A map $f : X \to Y$ between algebraic varieties is a \emph{regular map} (or \emph{morphism}) if, locally on $X$, its coordinate functions are given by polynomials.

With these notions in hand, we turn to algebraic groups.

\begin{definition}
    An \emph{algebraic group} $G$ is an algebraic variety equipped with a group structure such that the multiplication map $m : G \times G \to G$, $(g,h) \mapsto gh$, and the inversion map $i : G \to G$, $g \mapsto g^{-1}$, are regular morphisms of varieties.
\end{definition}

Standard examples of algebraic groups include the one-dimensional additive group $\mathbb{G}_a = k$ and the multiplicative group $\mathbb{G}_m = k \setminus \{0\}$. Furthermore, the general linear group $\GL_n(k)$ is an algebraic group, and any closed subgroup (under the Zariski topology) naturally inherits this structure. This yields a rich family of examples, such as the special linear group $\SL_n(k)$, the subgroups of diagonal or triangular matrices, and the classical symplectic and orthogonal groups ($\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}(k)$ and $\mathrm{O}_{m}(k)$), which are cut out by polynomial equations preserving specific bilinear forms.

\begin{proposition}
    Let $u : G \to H$ be a morphism of algebraic groups. The following are equivalent:
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item[(i)] $u$ is an isomorphism of groups;
        \item[(ii)] $u$ is an isomorphism of algebraic groups.
    \end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

Every algebraic group also has an associated Lie algebra; we omit the details of this construction here and instead refer the reader to \cite[Ch.\ 23]{tauvelLieAlgebrasAlgebraic2005} and \cite[Ch.\ 2]{milneLAG}.

When $k = \RR$ or $k = \bC$, an algebraic group also carries a natural Lie group structure, obtained by equipping it with the standard analytic topology as a subspace of $\RR^n$ or $\bC^n$ (see \cite[Ch.\ 3, Thm.\ 2.1]{milneLAG}). In this case, the Lie algebra of $G$ regarded as an algebraic group and its Lie algebra as a Lie group coincide.

\section{Finitely Generated Groups}

\begin{definition}
    A group $\Gamma$ is \emph{finitely generated} if it admits a finite subset $S \subseteq \Gamma$ such that every element of $\Gamma$ is a finite product of elements of $S$ and their inverses.
\end{definition}

Common examples include all finite groups, such as the cyclic groups $\ZZ/n\ZZ$, as well as fundamental infinite discrete groups like the integers $\ZZ$ and the free groups $F_n$ on $n$ generators. Other classical examples arise naturally from the study of symmetries, encompassing the symmetric and dihedral groups, and, more broadly, the family of Coxeter groups.

A prominent family of finitely generated groups is that of surface groups arising from Riemann surfaces.

\begin{definition}[Surface groups]
    Let $g, n \geqslant 0$. The \emph{surface group} of genus $g$ with $n$ punctures is
    \[
        \pi_{g,n} := \left\langle a_1, b_1, \dots, a_g, b_g, c_1, \dots, c_n \;\middle|\; \prod_{i=1}^g [a_i, b_i] = \prod_{j=1}^n c_j \right\rangle,
    \]
    where $[a_i, b_i] = a_i b_i a_i^{-1} b_i^{-1}$ for $i = 1, \dots, g$.

    When $n = 0$, this is the fundamental group of a closed surface of genus $g$. For $n \geqslant 1$, the defining relation shows that $\pi_{g,n}$ is a free group of rank $2g + n - 1$. Among these groups, only $\pi_{0,2} \cong \ZZ$ and the torus group $\pi_{1,0} \cong \ZZ^2$ are abelian; the groups $\pi_{0,0}$ and $\pi_{0,1}$ are trivial, and all higher-genus groups are non-abelian.
\end{definition}

The following theorem justifies the terminology \emph{surface group}.

\begin{theorem}
    Let $\Sigma_{g,n}$ be an orientable surface of genus $g$ with $n$ punctures. Then
    \[
        \pi_1(\Sigma_{g,n}) \cong \pi_{g,n}.
    \]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
    See \cite[Thm.\ 1]{casimiroCharacterVarietiesTheir2026}.
\end{proof}
